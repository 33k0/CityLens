# San José background for the staff chat

Short, widely documented background the assistant may use to suggest *why* a pattern in the data might exist. It is
context, not evidence: nothing here was computed from CityLens data, and staff should verify it before using it
publicly. The assistant must label anything from this file as background and pair it with what the app's data shows.

## participation
Research on local government participation in the US consistently finds that homeowners, older residents, higher
income residents and English speakers are over-represented in public comment, council meetings and written letters.
Renters move more often and have less time and stake in long processes. Residents with limited English face meetings,
agendas and comment forms that are mostly in English. Written comment also favors people who know the agenda calendar
and how to reach their council office.

## reporting
311 volume reflects both how many problems a place has and who uses 311. Heavy users tend to be residents who know the
system and expect a response. Some service types (bulky-item/junk pickup, graffiti, illegal dumping, vehicle concerns)
are high volume and concentrated where housing is dense and older, where many residents rent, and where dumping
hotspots exist. A low 311 rate does not always mean few problems.

## east-side
East San José grew quickly after World War II, much of it through the city's aggressive annexation program of the
1950s and 1960s, and became home to large Mexican-American and later Vietnamese communities. The Mayfair area
("Sal Si Puedes") is where César Chávez lived and began organizing in the early 1950s. Residents and advocates have long
argued that East Side neighborhoods received infrastructure and services later than other parts of the city.

## districts
San José voters moved city council elections from at-large to district seats in 1978, with the first district
elections in 1980. The change is generally described as a response to the city's west side and downtown interests
dominating an at-large council.

## redlining
Federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) maps from the 1930s graded parts of San José, including older areas
near downtown and the east side, as hazardous for lending. Mapped areas with low grades often show lasting differences
in homeownership and investment nationally; whether a given San José census tract matches an old graded area has to
be checked against the historical map.

## vietnamese
San José has one of the largest Vietnamese-American communities in the US, which grew after 1975. The Story Road
business district is commonly known as Little Saigon; a 2007–2008 dispute over officially naming it shows how
contested representation at City Hall has been for that community.

## language
A majority of San José residents speak a language other than English at home, with Spanish, Vietnamese, Chinese and
Tagalog among the most common. Language access (interpretation at meetings, translated notices) is a recurring issue in
public participation.

## crime-and-place
Census tract-level links between a neighborhood's makeup and 311 public-safety reports describe places, not people.
They are shaped by density, housing age, traffic corridors, policing and reporting habits. Never present them as a
trait of an ethnic or language group.
